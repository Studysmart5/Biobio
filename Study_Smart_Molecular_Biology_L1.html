    Molecular Biology & the Central Dogma

    What is Molecular Biology? علم الأحياء الجزيئي

    Molecular Biology: The science which seeks to explain the relationships between the structure and function of biological molecules and how these relationships contribute to the operation and control of biochemical all processes. Of principal interest in the macromocular complexes of DNA, RNA and protein and the processes of replication, transcription and translation Figure (1-1).

    Figure 1-1. The central dogma of molecular biology

    The arrows shown in the figure:

    Arrow in Fig. 1-1FromTo
    ReplicationDNADNA
    TranscriptionDNARNA

    Reverse transcription (dashed arrow)RNADNA
    ReplicationRNARNA

    2. Transcription → RNA

    Quick Review

    • Molecular biology links the structure of biological molecules to their function.
    • Main interest: DNA, RNA and protein, and the processes of replication, transcription and translation.
    • DNA → RNA = transcription; RNA → protein = translation; RNA → DNA = reverse transcription.

    Unit 2

    Some Terminology

    Genetic terms

    TermDefinition (as in the lecture)
    GenomeAn organism’s genetic material.
    GeneDiscrete units of hereditary information located on the chromosomes and consisting of DNA.
    GenotypeThe genetic makeup of an organism.
    PhenotypeThe physical expressed traits of an organism.
    Nucleic acidBiological molecules (RNA and DNA) that allow organisms to reproduce.

    Pseudo genes & Introns

    Pseudo genes are the DNA sequences that are related to the known genes that have lost their protein coding ability or the genes that are no longer expressed in the cell. Pseudo genes, which are also called ‘genomic fossils’, are non-functional due to mutations.

    Introns: Introns are the non-coding regions of a gene. They are the intervening sequences of an mRNA and are not translated into protein sequences.

    d-value & C-value

    d-value: The difference in the length between the long arm and the short arm of a chromosome, i.e., d = q − p, where q and p are the lengths of the long arm and the short arm of the chromosome, respectively.

    SymbolMeaning
    dd-value (difference in length between the arms)
    qlength of the long arm of the chromosome
    plength of the short arm of the chromosome

    ‘C-value enigma’ or ‘C-value paradox’. The total amount of DNA contained in a haploid genome is sometimes referred to as the ‘C-value’ for the species.

    Quick Review

    • Genome = genetic material; genotype = genetic makeup; phenotype = expressed traits.
    • Pseudo genes = ‘genomic fossils’, non-functional due to mutations.
    • Introns are non-coding and not translated.
    • d = q − p (long arm − short arm); C-value = DNA in a haploid genome.

    Unit 3

    Proteins

    Introduction to Proteins

    Definition: Proteins are linear macromolecules (polypeptides) made of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.

    Functions

    • Catalyze biochemical reactions (enzymes).
    • Transport small molecules, ions, or metals.
    • Involved in cell division, growth, and differentiation.
    • Control muscle movement, nerve impulses, blood clotting, and immune defense.
    • Mechanical roles in skin, bones, and blood vessels.

    Peptide Bonds

    Formation

    • Amino acids link together through peptide bonds (or amide bonds) between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another.
    • This creates a polypeptide chain.

    Polypeptide Directionality

    • Starts with an amino group (—NH2) at the N-terminus and ends with a carboxyl group (—COOH) at the C-terminus.
    • Sometimes the N-terminus is blocked by groups like acetyl groups.

    Levels of Protein Structure

    LevelDefinitionDetails
    Primary StructureThe sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide from the N-terminus to the C-terminus.Polypeptide Backbone: Composed of repeating α-carbon atoms and peptide bonds.
    Size: Polypeptide chains can range from 100 to 1500 amino acids, though longer or shorter chains exist.
    Secondary Structural UnitsRegions of a protein that form specific spatial structures.Common Structures:
    α-Helices: Spiral structure.
    β-Pleated Sheets: Flat, sheet-like structure.
    Tertiary StructureThe complete three-dimensional structure required for the protein's function.Formation: Based on the primary and secondary structures. Creates specific spatial relationships between amino acids far apart in the sequence.
    Quaternary StructureThe further folding and assembly of multiple polypeptide subunits into a functional protein.Function: Ensures correct subunit interactions for proper function.

    Example (secondary structure): Insulin is 57% α-helical, 6% β-pleated sheets, 10% β-turns, and 27% undefined regions.

    Nucleoproteins

    Definition: Complexes of proteins and nucleic acids (DNA or RNA).

    Examples

    ExampleComposition
    Ribosomes — Bacterial 70SComposed of 50S and 30S subunits.
    Ribosomes — Eukaryotic 80SComposed of 60S and 40S subunits.
    ChromatinContains DNA and histone proteins.
    VirusesOften contain nucleoprotein complexes.

    Protein Sizes and Shapes

    TypeDescriptionExamples
    Globular ProteinsCompact, spherical proteins.enzymes
    Fibrous ProteinsElongated, structural proteins.fibroin, keratin
    • Size Range: From a few thousand to several million Daltons.
    • Associated Materials: Some proteins have non-protein components like lipids, carbohydrates, or cofactors.

    Quick Review

    • Proteins = polypeptides of amino acids joined by peptide (amide) bonds.
    • Chain runs N-terminus (—NH2) → C-terminus (—COOH).
    • Primary → Secondary (α-helix, β-sheet) → Tertiary (3-D) → Quaternary (subunits).
    • Ribosomes: 70S = 50S + 30S (bacterial); 80S = 60S + 40S (eukaryotic).
    • Globular (enzymes) vs fibrous (fibroin, keratin).

    Unit 4

    Nucleotides: Phosphate, Sugar & Bases

    Introduction to Nucleotides and Nucleic Acids

    • Nucleic Acids: DNA and RNA are macromolecules crucial for storing and transmitting genetic information.
    • Nucleotides: The building blocks of DNA and RNA, involved in energy transfer, coenzymes, and metabolic regulation.

    Composition of Nucleotides

    1. Phosphate group
    2. Sugar (ribose or deoxyribose)
    3. Purine or pyrimidine base

    Phosphate Groups

    Definition: Phosphate groups in nucleotides can exist as:

    FormAs stated in the lectureExample
    MonophosphatesOne phosphate atomAMP
    DiphosphatesTwo phosphate atomsADP
    TriphosphatesThree phosphate atomsATP

    Significance: The number of phosphate groups impacts the nucleotide's role in energy storage and transfer.

    Sugar Residues in Nucleotides

    SugarFound inNucleoside naming

    DeoxyriboseDNA (β-D-deoxyribose)Deoxyribonucleosides: Nucleosides with deoxyribose (e.g., deoxyadenosine).

    Pyrimidine Bases

    Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T), and Uracil (U).

    BaseDistinguishing group
    Cytosine (C)Has an —NH2 group on C4.
    Thymine (T)Has a —CH3 group on C5.
    Uracil (U)Has an oxygen (O) on C4.

    Uracil is found in RNA, while thymine is found in DNA.

    Purine Bases

    Purines: Adenine (A) and Guanine (G).

    BaseDistinguishing feature
    Adenine (A)Has a double bond between N1 and C6.
    Guanine (G)Lacks this double bond.

    Role: These bases pair with pyrimidines to form the structure of DNA and RNA.

    • Nucleotide = phosphate + sugar + purine/pyrimidine base.
    • AMP / ADP / ATP = mono-, di-, triphosphate.
    • Ribose → RNA; deoxyribose → DNA.

    • Nucleoside = sugar + base (N-glycosidic bond); nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate.

    Nucleic Acids: Chain & Directionality

    Introduction to Nucleic Acids

    • Nucleic Acids: Composed of a series of nucleotides joined by phosphodiester bonds.
    • Nucleotides are linked between the 3ʹ carbon atom of one nucleotide and the 5ʹ carbon atom of the next.
    • Directionality: Nucleic acid sequences are written from the 5ʹ to 3ʹ direction.
    • Example: ATCG represents adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G) in a 5ʹ to 3ʹ sequence.

    Unit 6

    DNA Forms: B, A & Z

    Introduction to DNA Forms

    Overview: DNA can exist in three main conformations depending on environmental conditions and cellular context:

    • B-DNA (classic form)
    • A-DNA (compact form)
    • Z-DNA (left-handed form)

    B-Form DNA

    Watson & Crick Model (1953)

    • B-DNA is the most common form in cells, and it is a right-handed double helix.

    Structure

    • 10.5 base pairs per turn.
    • Helix diameter: 20 Å (2*10–6 mm).
    • Bases are spaced 0.36 nm (3.6 Å) apart.
    • Helical Turn: Repeats every 3.6 nm, with the sugar-phosphate backbone on the outside and base pairs on the inside.
    • Grooves: A major groove (wide) and a minor groove (narrow).

    A-Form DNA

    • A-DNA: Forms under low humidity conditions or when DNA is bound to RNA or RNA/RNA helices.
    • Compact Structure: 11 base pairs per turn (compared to 10.5 in B-DNA).
    • Helix diameter: 20 Å.
    • Features: Deep major groove and flat minor groove.
    • A-DNA is less common, occurring mainly in dehydrated conditions or experimentally in vitro.

    Z-Form DNA

    • Z-DNA: A unique left-handed helix, differing from the right-handed B- and A-DNA forms.
    • Zigzag Structure: The sugar-phosphate backbone forms a zigzag pattern.
    • Base pairs are spaced farther apart (0.77 nm).
    • High-Energy Form: Z-DNA has a greater distance between base pairs and is thermodynamically unstable.
    • Groove: A single groove with a higher density of negatively charged molecules.

    Formation and Stability of Z-DNA

    • Transition from B-DNA to Z-DNA: B-DNA, particularly GC-rich sequences, can convert to Z-DNA when the bases rotate 180 degrees.
    • Methylation: Cytosine methylation at position C5 promotes the transition to Z-DNA. This modification is common in specific regions of eukaryotic DNA.
    • Instability: Z-DNA is generally unstable, but the transition is more favorable when cytosine is methylated.
    1. B-DNA (GC-rich sequences)
    2. Bases rotate 180° (favoured by C5-methylated cytosine)
    3. Z-DNA

    Biological Roles of Z-DNA

    • Z-DNA in Gene Regulation: Z-DNA sequences are often located near promoter regions, where they stimulate transcription.
    • Z-DNA Binding Proteins: Four families of Z-DNA binding proteins have been identified.
    • These proteins have defined three-dimensional structures that specifically interact with Z-DNA, further influencing transcription regulation.

    DNA Conformations (summary slide)

    DNA can exist in three different forms:

    • A-DNA: More compact form.
    • B-DNA: The common form, right-handed helix.
    • Z-DNA: Left-handed helix.

    Variability: DNA’s structure can change depending on environmental conditions.

    Quick Review

    • B-DNA: right-handed, 10.5 bp/turn, 20 Å, 0.36 nm spacing, turn every 3.6 nm.
    • A-DNA: compact, 11 bp/turn, deep major + flat minor groove, low humidity.
    • Z-DNA: left-handed zigzag, 0.77 nm spacing, single groove, unstable.
    • GC-rich B-DNA → Z-DNA (bases rotate 180°), promoted by C5 cytosine methylation.
    • Z-DNA near promoters stimulates transcription; 4 families of Z-DNA binding proteins.

    Unit 7

    The Double Helix: Pairing, Chargaff & Grooves

    Hydrogen Bonds Between Bases

    • Base Pairing: A purine (adenine or guanine) pairs with a pyrimidine (thymine or cytosine) through hydrogen bonds.
    PairHydrogen bonds
    A-T PairTwo hydrogen bonds (2)
    G-C PairThree hydrogen bonds (3)

    Complementarity: Base pairs always form between adenine and thymine, or guanine and cytosine, ensuring the specificity of DNA structure.

    Chargaff’s Rule

    Chargaff’s Observations: The amount of adenine (A) equals thymine (T), and the amount of guanine (G) equals cytosine (C) in DNA, regardless of the species.

    Significance: This base pairing rule is key to DNA structure and replication.

    Discovery of DNA Structure

    • Watson and Crick (1953): Proposed the double helix model of DNA.
    • This structure explained how genetic information is stored and replicated.

    Key Mechanisms

    • Storage: Linear sequence of bases encodes genetic information.
    • Replication: DNA structure allows for faithful replication.

    DNA Grooves

    DNA twisting creates two grooves:

    GrooveWidth
    Major Groove22 Å wide
    Minor Groove12 Å wide

    Function: The major groove is more accessible for proteins, such as transcription factors, which bind to DNA and regulate gene expression.

    DNA Double Strand and Double Helix

    Double-Stranded DNA

    • DNA consists of two complementary strands arranged in a double helix.
    • The nucleotide bases are arranged in pairs (A-T and G-C) between the two strands.

    Base Pairing

    • The sequence of one strand (5ʹ to 3ʹ) is complementary to the sequence of the other strand (3ʹ to 5ʹ).
    • The specificity of base pairing is essential for DNA structure and function.

    Structure of B-DNA

    B-Form of DNA

    • Most common form of DNA, a right-handed helix.
    • Distance between base pairs: 0.36 nm (3.6 Å).
    • Helical Turn: Repeats every 3.6 nm, with 10.5 base pairs per turn.
    • Genetic Information: Determined by the sequence of base pairs (bp).

    Structure

    • The sugar-phosphate backbone is on the outside, while the base pairs lie inside the helix.

    Note on the source: the slide text gives a base-pair distance of 0.36 nm (3.6 Å), while the labels inside Fig. 1-5 show 0.34 nm and 3.4 nm. Both are kept here exactly as they appear in the lecture.

    Quick Review

    • Purine pairs with pyrimidine: A–T (2 H-bonds), G–C (3 H-bonds).
    • Chargaff: A = T and G = C in DNA, regardless of species.
    • Watson & Crick (1953) proposed the double helix.
    • Major groove 22 Å (protein access, e.g., transcription factors); minor groove 12 Å.
    • Strands are complementary: 5ʹ→3ʹ pairs with 3ʹ→5ʹ.
